India has spent the last decade as a consumer of artificial intelligence built somewhere else. Every
prompt sent to a global model is, in a quiet way, a small export of capital and context. Sarvam AI's
latest announcement is the most serious attempt yet to change that.

The company is building a **trillion-parameter model**, aimed squarely at the frontier models - ChatGPT,
Gemini and Claude. Ambition alone is not the story. The interesting part is what sits underneath it.

## Four things the announcement actually says

- **Global reach.** Sarvam is opening a US office, recruiting Indian-origin AI researchers to work on
  coding, cybersecurity, scientific research and physics simulation. Talent, not just compute.
- **Unmatched efficiency.** Their voice systems run **four to five times cheaper** than comparable global
  models, with state-of-the-art English and vernacular speech recognition. Cost per token is the metric
  that decides what a business can actually deploy.
- **Proven scale.** They have already processed over **325 million minutes** of voice calls, and their
  existing 100-billion-parameter model lands within **four to five percentage points** of far larger
  global models.
- **Sovereignty as an argument, not a slogan.** Co-founder Pratyush Kumar makes the point plainly:
  depending entirely on imported tokens means losing both capital and data to companies overseas.
  Sarvam is building a domestic token factory so that India owns its own AI future.

## Why this matters beyond the model itself

A trillion-parameter model is a headline. The durable value is the **plumbing** around it: cheaper
inference, voice that handles Indian languages properly, and data that stays inside the country.

That last point is the one enterprises should read twice. For banking, healthcare and public services,
data residency has been the barrier that stalled generative AI at the planning stage. A domestic model
running on domestic infrastructure removes the objection entirely. You can read more about how that
shift is playing out in [Claude's in-country processing](/blog/claude-in-country-india.html).

## The part that does not solve itself

Here is the distinction worth holding on to. A frontier model built in India is a **capability** advance.
It is not a **capability** transfer to your team.

The tool being available and affordable does not mean your people can direct it well. The gap most
organisations are sitting in is not access to models - it is the ability to frame a problem, question an
output, and know when to overrule it. Technology scales capacity. Human judgement decides what that
capacity is pointed at.

That is the same point Sarvam's own framing makes: infrastructure removes the roadblock, but the
competitive advantage comes from how effectively teams direct these tools.

## What I would do next

If your organisation has been waiting for a reason to move on generative AI, the two objections that
mattered most - cost and data residency - are both weakening at the same time.

The useful question is no longer "which model?" It is **"who is going to direct it, and are they
equipped to?"** Start with the teams whose judgement carries the most consequence - quality, clinical,
financial - and build capability there first.
