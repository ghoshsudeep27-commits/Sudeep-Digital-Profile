You got the title before you got the manual.

Nobody hands a first-time manager a rulebook. You inherit a team, a target and a set of
expectations, and somewhere around week three you discover that the real job has almost nothing
in common with the one you were promoted from. The skills that got you here - being the fastest,
the most reliable, the one who never needed help - are not the skills the new role asks for.

Dr. Ram Charan has spent decades advising boards and chief executives. In a column this week, he
sets out five things a new leader should focus on. Here they are, in plain terms, with the reasoning
behind each.

## 1. Go deep, and keep learning 🧠

Your first job is to understand the ecosystem you have joined - the company, its market, its
customers, and the way decisions actually get made. That means talking to people: employees,
customers, shareholders. Not a listening tour as a gesture, but a genuine audit of what is working
well and where the gaps are.

That understanding is what your strategy will be built on later. Which is why the learning does not
stop at ninety days. The leaders who struggle are rarely the ones who knew too little on day one;
they are the ones who stopped finding out on day sixty.

## 2. Assess your team honestly, and place people right 👥

Put the right people in the right roles. Read their strengths, see what each person is genuinely
good at, and do not flinch from the uncomfortable question about who is in the wrong seat.

Charan's point about crisis is the one worth underlining: an honest assessment brings clarity, and
clarity gives people confidence. A team can absorb difficult news. What it cannot absorb is a leader
who will not look.

## 3. Clear the pending decisions ⏳

Find the decisions that have been sitting unresolved, and resolve them.

Delay is not neutral. It carries cost while it sits there, and it quietly erodes your authority -
because a team learns very quickly whether the person in charge actually decides anything. Decisiveness,
even imperfect decisiveness, builds trust and commitment. Indecision builds a queue.

## 4. Build a clear vision, and carry everyone with it 🎯

Develop a vision your leadership team genuinely shares, then communicate it so clearly that every
person can see their own part in it. Direction first, detail second.

A vision that lives only in the leader's head is not a vision; it is a private opinion. The work is
in the communication, and in repeating it more often than feels necessary.

## 5. Get a firm grip on finance and operations 📊

Understand the company's financial health and how it actually earns money - cash flow, the cost
levers, the margins. This is not accountancy for its own sake. That grip is what lets you forecast
honestly and plan strategically, instead of reacting to whatever number arrives next.

## The first 90 days, in sequence 🗓️

The order matters as much as the list. In the opening months: grasp the product and the market
quickly, understand who your customers are and where investment is going, learn the strengths of
the people you have inherited, and start creating value.

> Talk to your customers, employees and shareholders. It will show you what is working well and
> where the gaps are.

## Why these five are human skills 🤝

Here is the part I would add, from the training room. 💡

None of these five are tool skills. They are human skills - listening, judging, deciding,
communicating. An AI assistant can draft the strategy document, summarise the customer feedback and
build the forecast model. It cannot sit with a team that has just lost a colleague to a reorganisation
and earn their trust. It cannot look someone in the eye and tell them they are in the wrong seat.

The tools make the analysis faster. They do not make the judgement, and they do not carry the
relationship. For a new leader, that gap is the whole job.

## What actually helps in the first quarter ✅

Three things I have seen work, repeatedly:

- 📅 **Book the conversations.** Diarise the listening before the calendar fills with delivery. Twenty conversations in the first month beats a strategy away-day in month three.
- 🗂️ **Keep a decision log.** Every pending decision, who owns it, when it will be closed. It turns point three from good advice into a habit.
- 📈 **Learn the numbers yourself.** Not the deck someone prepares for you - the underlying model, the cash position, the two or three levers that actually move margin.
